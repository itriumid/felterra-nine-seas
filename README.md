# Felterra: Nine Seas

A pirate sailing game set on Felterra, an Earth where cats are the apex species. Sailors have
seven seas and cats have nine lives, so a world of cats has nine seas.

Inspired by Tradewinds, but turned toward the pirate's side of the trade, and built around
sailing that behaves like the real thing: true and apparent wind, points of sail, ships that
can't sail into the wind, square-riggers that can't point as high as a sloop, and a bow wave
that won't let a hull go much past its hull speed.

**Status:** a prototype. The first of the nine seas (the Nusantara seas, from Aceh to Makassar)
in one view you can zoom from the whole chart to the deck, two ships, merchant and navy traffic,
and no cats yet.

## The world map

The chart is the real coastline and seafloor of the Nusantara seas, one kilometer to a pixel:
Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, Java, Borneo and the islands between, the shallow Sunda Shelf,
and the deep water beyond it. The winds are the real monsoons. From December to March they
blow from the northeast north of the equator and from the northwest over the Java Sea; from
June to September they reverse; in between they're light and wander. The game starts at dawn
on the 15th of January in Batavia.

Click anywhere on the sea, or on a port, and your crew sails there: around the land and,
when the wind is against you, beating to windward. Press A or D (or the arrow keys) to take
the helm yourself; that cancels the course. Zoom with the mouse wheel or minus and equals, from
the whole chart down to the deck, where every ship is drawn at its true size with its sails set
to the real wind. The clock runs at ten minutes a second, the same for everyone.

Merchants trade between the ports and navy squadrons patrol their stations, all sailing the
same physics as you, so a merchant bound east into the July monsoon zigzags up the coast just
as a real one did. Sails within 20 kilometers are reported as sighted.

## Weather

On top of the monsoon, weather moves across the chart. Squalls are sudden blows a few
kilometers to fifteen across that rise to 30 knots or more and die away within a couple of
hours. They come thickest when the monsoons change, and on southwest-monsoon nights in the
Malacca Strait, where the Sumatras sweep across from Sumatra. Gales are great areas of strong
wind a hundred kilometers or more across, blowing for a day or two at the height of a monsoon.
On the chart they show as dark, rain-streaked cloud drifting with the wind.

Each rig carries full sail only up to a certain wind: about 21 knots for a sloop and 25 for a
brig. Above that, too much canvas strains the rigging and, slowly, the hull. Your crew shortens
sail on its own while following a course, but never sets more than you ordered; at the helm,
press S. Every other ship reefs for the weather too. In a battle, heeling too far strains the
rigging the same way.

Rain hides ships. In clear weather a lookout makes out a sail 25 kilometers off; in the heart
of a squall, barely a kilometer and a half. A pirate or a navy ship hunting you keeps after you
by sight and by word, but lose yourself in the rain and it can only sail to where you were last
seen. If it doesn't find you there within a few hours, it gives up, and won't take up the hunt
again for half a day. Lying still in the rain doesn't help: they search where they saw you.

## Ports and trade

Click a port and your crew sails there and moors, or, within five kilometers of one (its
harbor), press M or furl all sail and the crew brings her in. Moored, the port
opens:

- **Market:** nine goods the Nusantara seas traded around 1700, from rice and salt to cloves
  and nutmeg, bought and sold in reals. A good costs least where it's produced and more the
  further it has to be carried, so neighboring ports keep close prices and the money is in the
  long hauls. Every ton you buy raises a port's price and every ton you sell lowers it; stocks
  recover over the following days.
- **Shipyard:** repair the hull, haul out and scrape the barnacles that slow a ship over weeks
  at sea, or trade your ship in for another class.
- **Tavern:** sign sailors on or pay them off. Wages fall due every day, unpaid sailors
  desert, and a ship short of its minimum crew can't carry all its sail.

Cargo weighs the ship down for real: a loaded hull is heavier, slower to answer the helm,
deeper in the water and steadier under sail. The world keeps its clock while you're in port.

## Pirates

Pirate sloops sail out of lairs among the Riau islands, Belitung and the Karimata islands, lie
in wait along the trade routes, and take the merchants they outnumber; the markets feel it. The
navy hunts them down. They'll come for you too if your crew looks small enough: broadsides to
soften you up, then grapples and boarders. Lose that fight and they strip your hold; beat them
off and they've lost their best hands. Up close, the weather gauge matters: a pirate to windward
chooses its moment, one to leeward has to claw up to you.

Fighting pirates is honest work. Taking or sinking one earns renown and honor, and a bounty that
the harbor master pays at the next port.

## Renown and honor

Renown is how widely you're known: captures, victories and trade all build it. Honor is how
you're known: attacking lawful ships costs some, sinking them instead of taking them costs a
lot, and it drifts back slowly as deeds are forgotten. A famous captain draws more sailors to the
taverns, and merchants strike sooner to a famous one, far sooner to one known for cruelty. Fall
below -20 honor and every navy ship near enough comes hunting you; in port you're safe.

## Battle bubbles

Click a ship to give chase. Chasers steer for where their quarry will be, not where it is. Close to 3 kilometers and a battle bubble opens: the view zooms in,
time inside runs four times faster than real, and the full sailing physics takes over. You
handle the rudder, sails and trim yourself; merchants run, using the wind as well as they can,
and hulls that meet collide with all the momentum they carry and the damage that goes
with it.

Guns fire in broadsides: Space fires every gun on the side facing the nearest ship, each trained
on her as far as its port allows and laid for her range. Turn your side to the enemy, as real
ships did. A broadside takes a minute to reload, longer short-handed (four sailors a gun), and C
changes the shot: round shot holes hulls, chain shot cuts rigging so a ship can carry less sail,
grapeshot sweeps a deck at close range. Balls scatter more the further they fly, smoke drifts
off on the wind, and a ship with no hull left goes down with everything aboard. A sloop's
six-pounders barely dent a brig: cut her rigging, sweep her deck, and board.

To take a ship, get alongside her, within a few meters and moving with her, and press G to
throw the grapples. Then it's numbers, by Lanchester's square law: each side loses fighters
in proportion to the other side's, so a big crew wins fast and cheaply, which is why pirate
sloops sailed crammed. Merchants outnumbered two to one strike their colors at once; navy crews
fight. Boarders who fall below four in ten of those who went over are thrown back. Take what
you want from a ship that has struck, then cast off; or take her as a prize. A prize crew of
your own sailors (her minimum crew, or as many as you can spare) sails her in company with you,
keeping station astern, and into port, where you can sell her and her cargo or move into her
and sell your old ship. Prize crews draw their wages like any other sailor. Sail more than 6 kilometers clear
and the bubble closes. The world outside doesn't wait: a bubble's minutes are the world's
hours, as they'd have to be with other players sharing it.

Coastlines and depths come from [Natural Earth](https://www.naturalearthdata.com), which is
in the public domain. To rebuild the chart, download `ne_10m_land`, `ne_10m_minor_islands`,
`ne_10m_bathymetry_K_200` and `ne_10m_bathymetry_I_2000` from the `geojson` directory of
[natural-earth-vector](https://github.com/nvkelso/natural-earth-vector) into one directory, then:

```bash
python3 tools/build_world_map.py <that directory> world/nusantara.png
```

## Saving

Your voyage is saved whenever you moor in a port and when you close the game, and the game
carries on from it next time. Saves wait out battles: a bubble lasts minutes, and you'll be back
on the map soon enough. To abandon a voyage and start again, run `godot --path . -- --new-voyage`.

The save is one file, `voyage.json`, in your system's place for application data, under
"Felterra Nine Seas" (on Windows, `%APPDATA%`; on macOS, `~/Library/Application Support`; on
Linux, `~/.local/share`).

## How the sailing works

The simulation lives in `simulation/` and doesn't depend on anything on screen:

- `wind.gd`: a trade wind from the east-northeast, with gusts that drift downwind and a
  direction that wanders.
- `ship.gd`: apparent wind, lift and drag on the sails, keel and hull resistance, heel, and a
  rudder that only works while water flows past it. The comment at the top walks through the
  model.
- `ship_design.gd`: the numbers that make a sloop a sloop and a brig a brig.
- `polar.gd`: each ship's steady speed at every angle to the wind.

Everything is in real units (meters, kilograms, newtons), stepped 60 times per second of game
time. Speeding up time runs more steps, never longer ones, so a ship sails the same at any
speed.

The views in `game/` draw the ships from the simulation instead of from sprites, onto a
640 by 360 screen scaled up by whole numbers, so the sails always show their real trim and
every pixel stays the same size.

## Development

The font, Itrium Pixel, comes from [its own repository](https://github.com/itriumid/pixel-font)
as a git submodule, so clone with `--recurse-submodules`, or run `git submodule update --init`
after cloning.

```bash
# Run the tests (import once first on a fresh checkout)
godot --headless --path . --import
godot --headless --path . --script tests/run_tests.gd

# Print the speed polars, for tuning
godot --headless --path . --script tools/print_polar.gd

# Rebuild the polar table the world map sails by (after any change to the sailing model)
godot --headless --path . --script tools/build_polars.gd

# Rebuild the port-to-port route table (after changing the chart, ports or navigation)
godot --headless --path . --script tools/build_routes.gd
```

The tests check behavior a sailor would recognize: the sloop's best angle upwind is about 45°,
the brig's about 60°, nothing sails into the wind, a stopped ship can't steer, and so on.
